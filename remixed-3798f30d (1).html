The trip you've planned is essentially a single thread following the spine of Asia: down the Himalayas, across the Tibetan plateau, along the Silk Road's defile, and over the Pamirs into Central Asia. Every leg has its own colour, climate, and cuisine. Below is the shape of it.

PrologueI.

Kathmandu, the threshold

Where the trip stops being a plan and starts being a journey. Marigold-strewn alleys, butter-lamp dusk, and one last hot shower before the plateau.

"You sit on a rooftop in Thamel, the sun goes red behind the hills, and you realise — this is where it begins."
Day 0 · Kathmandu, 1,400 m

Kathmandu is more than a launching pad. It is the threshold city — where the air is still warm and noisy and the streets smell of marigold, diesel and cardamom, and where every traveller before you has paused to gather themselves before walking up. Spend three or four days. Sleep late. Drink masala chai on rooftops. Watch the eyes of Bhairava blacken with smoke in Durbar Square at dusk. Walk the kora around Boudhanath — three times, slowly, with the grandmothers who have been doing it every evening for forty years.

This is also where the Tibet permit gets stamped, the Chinese group visa is issued at the embassy in town, and the agency briefs you on the road north. Use the time. Buy a down jacket if you don't have one. Get cash. Eat one last plate of crispy momo from a street vendor.

Why this leg is worth it

It calibrates you. Everything that follows — the rarefied air of the plateau, the silences of the desert, the rough comfort of an Osh chaikhana — will land harder because you started in the warm chaos of a Hindu-Buddhist capital where every alley is a shrine.

Cultural
Boudhanath at dusk

Largest stupa in Nepal, ringed by Tibetan refugee monasteries. The kora at sunset, with butter lamps lighting one by one, is one of the most quietly moving things you will see all year.

Historical
Patan Durbar Square

The most beautiful of the three royal squares — Newari woodcarving, Krishna temples, the Royal Palace, and a museum that ranks among Asia's finest. Quieter than Kathmandu's Durbar.

Landscape
Nagarkot dawn

An hour east of the city, a ridge that on a clear morning shows the entire Himalayan crest from Annapurna to Everest. Stay overnight, set the alarm for 5:30. It is worth losing the sleep.

Culinary
Momo, dal bhat, sel roti

Buff momos at a Tibetan-run shop in Boudha. Dal bhat — the all-day Nepali plate. Newari food (chatamari, choila) at Bhojan Griha or Honacha. A tongba — millet beer drunk through a bamboo straw.

Practical · Kathmandu

When to be here
Late March or early April, just before heading north — visa processing takes 4 days at the Chinese embassy.
Stay in
Patan or Boudha, not Thamel. Quieter, more atmospheric, easier mornings.
Spend
~€40–70 per night in a charming guesthouse; €5–8 per meal.
Don't forget
Two full passport copies, six photos each (you'll need them in Tibet, Xinjiang and at borders).
A moment to hold

Sit at Boudhanath at the end of your last evening here. Hold each other's hand. The stupa's eyes are looking north — the same direction you are about to go.

Chapter TwoII.

Across the Himalayas

Eight days on the Friendship Highway. Bhote Koshi gorges, Gyirong border, Tingri's high steppe, Everest's north face, and Yamdrok Lake's impossible turquoise.

"On the third morning the air thins and the road begins climbing in earnest, and you don't speak much, because there is nothing to say."
Day 3 · Tingri, 4,300 m

This is the leg that earns the trip its title. Eight days from Kathmandu's heat and dust, up through the Bhote Koshi gorge to the Gyirong border, and then onto the great Tibetan plateau. The road climbs and the colours shift: green to brown, brown to ochre, ochre to white. By Tingri you are at 4,300 metres and your guesthouse will be cold and the stars will be brighter than you knew stars could be.

From Tingri you make the short detour to Rongbuk Monastery and the Tibetan side of Everest Base Camp — at 5,200 metres, the highest place either of you will probably ever stand. Then back onto the Friendship Highway: Shigatse, the Tashilhunpo Monastery (seat of the Panchen Lama), the gentle pilgrim town of Gyantse, and the high pass at Karo La where Yamdrok Lake suddenly opens below you, scorpion-shaped and the colour of melted glaciers.

Why this leg is worth it

You will never forget the moment Everest comes out of the cloud. That is reason enough. The ascent itself — slow, deliberate, at the pace of a four-wheel-drive on a high road — is also a kind of meditation that no plane can give you.

Landscape
Everest North Face

From Rongbuk, the north face stands directly in front of you with no foreground — a sheer 3,500 m wall. Sunrise is the time. The cold is brutal and worth it.

Landscape
Yamdrok Lake

One of three sacred lakes of Tibet. The first sight from Karo La pass, with the lake the colour of a Florentine sky, is the single most photographed view in Tibet for good reason.

Cultural
Tashilhunpo Monastery

The seat of the Panchen Lama, founded 1447. Best in the morning when monks debate in the courtyard — a centuries-old ritual of clapping, gesturing, sparring with logic.

Culinary
Tsampa, butter tea, tingmo

Tsampa (roasted barley flour) kneaded with butter tea is the staple of the plateau. Tingmo are steamed bread rolls served with everything. A bowl of thukpa at altitude tastes better than it has any right to.

Practical · The Crossing

Tour cost
~€1,000–1,500 pp for the standard 8-day Kathmandu→Lhasa overland (private car, guide, hotels, all permits).
Altitude protocol
Slow ascent, lots of water, no alcohol nights 1–3. Diamox in your kit. Don't be heroic — listen to your body.
What to pack
Down jacket, thermals, sun protection (UV is brutal), lip balm, a wool buff. Layers above all.
Best window
April–early June. Tibet closes to foreigners February–March each year — plan around that.
A moment to hold

First light at Rongbuk, hands inside your sleeves, breath visible, Everest turning gold from the top down. No words.

Chapter ThreeIII.

Lhasa, the holy city

Three or four days at 3,656 metres. The Potala on its hill, the Jokhang at dawn, the Barkhor in late afternoon, sweet tea-houses, and the way altitude makes every walk a little holy.

"At dawn the pilgrims circle the Jokhang, prayer wheels turning, robes burgundy in the rising sun, and you are entirely surplus to requirements — which is the point."
Day 9 · Lhasa, 3,656 m

Lhasa is the holy city, the destination of every Tibetan pilgrim's life. You arrive after eight days on the road already half-acclimatised, which is a kindness — most travellers fly in and spend their first 24 hours dizzy on a hotel bed. You will not. You will walk straight into the Barkhor, the ancient pilgrim circuit around the Jokhang, and you will join its slow clockwise current.

Three days is enough. The Potala Palace — the Dalai Lama's winter residence until 1959, now a museum-monument of impossible scale, thirteen stories of white-and-red mud-brick rising from the Red Hill — costs an entry slot booked the day before. The Jokhang at dawn, when it is full of prostrating pilgrims and the smell of butter lamps is overwhelming, is its own kind of sacrament. In the afternoons, drink sweet milk tea in a Barkhor tea-house and watch the city pass.

Why this leg is worth it

Almost no city on earth is still fundamentally organised around a single religious heart the way Lhasa is. Walking the Barkhor at any time of day, you are inside something living — not a museum, not a heritage site, but an actual practice that has been ongoing for thirteen centuries.

Cultural
The Jokhang at dawn

Tibet's most sacred temple. Founded 7th century. Go at 7am — outside it, hundreds of pilgrims will be doing full-body prostrations on the flagstones. Inside, the Jowo Shakyamuni statue, brought from Tang dynasty China.

Cultural
Sera Monastery debates

Every afternoon (except Sundays), monks gather under the willow trees to debate Buddhist philosophy, with elaborate hand-claps to punctuate arguments. Free, riveting, theatrical.

Historical
The Potala

13 stories, 1,000+ rooms, 200,000 statues. You walk it in a guided 90 minutes. It is more powerful from outside, at sunset, with butter lamps catching gold on the roof.

Culinary
Sweet tea & momo

Sweet milk tea (cha ngarmo) is the social drink of Lhasa — order a thermos at any tea-house and stay for hours. Eat at Tashi I or Makye Ame on Barkhor — yak burgers, momos, thenthuk, all good.

Practical · Lhasa

Days needed
3–4. More if you want Namtso Lake (a 6,000 m pass north of the city — overnight trip).
Guide rules
Officially required for tourist sites. In practice, free time in Barkhor and tea-houses is yours.
Stay near
Barkhor area — Yak Hotel or House of Shambhala for atmosphere. Walk everywhere.
Onward
Have your agency book the Lhasa→Xining train ticket as part of the tour. Don't try to do it solo.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon on the Barkhor. The sun catches the gold roofs of the Jokhang. Pilgrims, monks, soldiers, tourists, all moving clockwise. You go with them.

Chapter FourIV.

The Sky Train

21 hours on the highest railway in the world. Lhasa to Xining over the Tanggula Pass at 5,072 metres, through Hoh Xil, sleeping in oxygen-fed carriages while the plateau slides past.

"You wake at 4am with the carriage gently rocking, look out the window, and there is the full moon over Hoh Xil with nothing else for two hundred kilometres in any direction."
Lhasa → Xining · Train Z8982 · 1,956 km

The Qinghai-Tibet Railway is the highest in the world and one of the great engineering feats of the last century — built on permafrost across some of the coldest, emptiest land on the planet. The carriages are pressurised. There is an oxygen outlet at every berth. You will not need it, but it is comforting to know it is there.

The Z8982 leaves Lhasa at 09:25. This is the train you want: it puts you across the Tanggula Pass — at 5,072 metres, the highest point of any railway anywhere — in late afternoon light. By dusk you are in Hoh Xil, the high cold desert where Tibetan antelope and wild yak still roam. You sleep through the gentle descent into Qinghai, and wake into the dry valleys around Golmud. By breakfast the landscape has become Central Asian. By 06:25 you are in Xining and on the Silk Road.

Why this leg is worth it

Trains like this do not exist elsewhere. It is not just transport — it is a long, low, panoramic farewell to the plateau. Take the soft sleeper. Buy the snacks. Open the curtain. Don't sleep until Hoh Xil is dark.

Landscape
Tanggula Pass

5,072 m. The highest railway point on earth. A single weather station, a few prayer flags, and absolutely nothing else. The train pauses; passengers crowd the windows.

Landscape
Hoh Xil & Cona Lake

UNESCO-listed wilderness. Watch for Tibetan antelope, wild ass, and the occasional bear. Cona Lake reflects the sky like polished steel.

Practical
Soft sleeper

Four berths per compartment, a door that closes, plenty of space. ~€100 pp. Hard sleeper (six berths, open) is fine but social. Worth the upgrade for 21 hours.

Culinary
Train picnic

Buy your supplies in Lhasa: dried yak jerky, tsampa biscuits, flask of butter tea, oranges, instant noodles. The dining car works but the picnic is the experience.

Practical · The Sky Train

The right train
Z8982 dep. Lhasa 09:25, arr. Xining 06:25. Daytime over Tanggula. Avoid Z8992 (evening departure — you sleep through the best scenery).
Booking
Through your Tibet agency at the same time as the tour. Foreign passports are clunky on 12306.cn. Service fee CNY 200–500 is normal.
Cost
Soft sleeper ~€100, hard sleeper ~€65. No Tibet permit needed to leave — only to enter.
Comfort
Carriages have Western and squat toilets, washbasins, hot water. No showers. Bring wet wipes and a small towel.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon, somewhere past Tanggula. You and Micky pressed against the window. The plateau gold under low light. A herd of wild ass running parallel to the tracks for half a minute, then gone.

Chapter FiveV.

The Hexi Corridor

A narrow defile between two deserts and two mountain ranges. The Silk Road's chokepoint, where every empire's caravans, monks, and exiles funnelled through. Now: bullet trains and rainbow mountains.

"This is the road every empire walked. The Han, the Tang, the Mongols, Marco Polo. You're walking it now, and it has not yet finished."
Xining → Jiayuguan · ~1,500 km

The Hexi Corridor is the Silk Road's defining geography — a narrow strip of habitable land squeezed between the Tibetan Plateau to the south and the Gobi to the north. For two thousand years, every caravan from Chang'an heading west had to thread this needle. The route still does that. So do you, but on a high-speed train at 250 km/h.

Xining is your launching pad — pause for a day at Kumbum Monastery (the great Tibetan Buddhist gompa, birthplace of Tsongkhapa) and try a bowl of Lanzhou-style hand-pulled beef noodles. Then west to Zhangye for the rainbow Danxia landforms — striped sandstone ridges that look impossibly painted, especially in late afternoon light. Onward to Jiayuguan: the western terminus of the Ming Great Wall, where exiles wept on their way out of China and where the desert genuinely begins.

Why this leg is worth it

Geography becomes legible here. You can finally see why this trip exists — why these particular cities, in this particular line, mattered. The Hexi Corridor turns the Silk Road from a metaphor into a route you are physically following.

Landscape
Zhangye Danxia

Striped sandstone the colours of a sunset. Layers of red, orange, ochre and white folded over each other across whole hillsides. Late afternoon, with the sun low — that's when it sings.

Historical
Jiayuguan Fort

The "First and Greatest Pass under Heaven" — westernmost guard tower of the Ming Wall. Exiles tossed a stone at the wall as they left; if it bounced back, they'd return. Many didn't.

Cultural
Kumbum Monastery (Ta'er Si)

One of Tibetan Buddhism's six great Gelug monasteries, near Xining. Famous for yak-butter sculptures of staggering intricacy, remade for the new year and then allowed to melt.

Culinary
Lanzhou beef noodles

The dish. Hand-pulled to your specified thickness, in a clear bone broth with chilli oil, coriander, white radish, and tender beef. Eat one for breakfast. Eat one for lunch. Don't apologise.

Practical · Hexi Corridor

How to move
High-speed rail throughout. Xining → Zhangye 2.5h, Zhangye → Jiayuguan 2h, Jiayuguan → Dunhuang ~4h or overnight slow train.
Days
1 Xining, 1 Zhangye, 1 Jiayuguan, then onward — call it a week including travel days.
Booking trains
Trip.com app works for foreigners. Book 5–7 days ahead. Second-class seats are fine and cheap.
Pay
Set up Alipay's "TourPass" before arriving in China. Cards almost never work; cash is awkward.
A moment to hold

Last light on the striped hills outside Zhangye. The colours don't make sense and you stop trying to make them.

Chapter SixVI.

Dunhuang, where the desert meets the Buddha

An oasis in the Gobi. A thousand years of Buddhist art carved into sandstone cliffs. A crescent-shaped lake against the dunes. Camels at sunset. The single most photogenic stop on the entire route.

"You climb the dune at sunset, the wind rises, and the lake below catches a ribbon of gold. The sand sings under your feet. This is what oasis means."
Day 18 · Dunhuang

Dunhuang is the heart of the Silk Road, and it does not disappoint. For a millennium it was the crossroads where Buddhist monks from India, traders from Persia, scholars from Chang'an, and Sogdian merchants all converged. They left behind one of the great cultural treasures of the world — the Mogao Caves: 492 sandstone grottoes carved into a cliff over a thousand years, every interior surface painted in mineral pigments still impossibly vivid.

And just outside the city, the Singing Sand Mountains — proper Saharan dunes — surround a tiny crescent-shaped freshwater lake that has not dried up in two thousand years. The contrast is deranged. Rent a camel at golden hour. Climb the highest dune. Watch the lake go from blue to gold to violet. This is the moment you will reach for when, two years from now, you try to remember why you took six months off.

Why this leg is worth it

Mogao alone is worth the trip — it is to Buddhist art what Sistine is to Renaissance painting. Add the Crescent Lake and dunes, the night markets, the apricot wine. Dunhuang earns its three days more easily than anywhere else on the route.

Cultural
Mogao Caves

492 grottoes, 45,000 m² of murals, 2,000 sculptures. You'll see 8 caves on a 2-hour guided tour with timed-entry. Book the English tour. The Library Cave's history (sealed 1000 years, opened 1900) is the subplot.

Landscape
Crescent Lake & Mingsha

A spring-fed lake against 100m sand dunes, kept liquid by aquifer for 2,000 years. Climb the dune at sunset; the sand "sings" (a low rumbling hum) when you slide down.

Historical
Yumen Pass & the Jade Gate

An afternoon's drive west — the actual western customs post of Han dynasty China. Mud-brick remnants on a windswept plain. Hauntingly empty. Where China ended.

Culinary
Shazhou Night Market

Local apricot wine, donkey-meat dumplings (donkey is the local specialty — try them), lamb skewers, naan bread, hand-pulled noodles. Wander, eat small things, repeat.

Practical · Dunhuang

Days
3 minimum. One for Mogao. One for the dunes. One for Yumen Pass and the night market.
Mogao tickets
Book online 2–7 days ahead via the official site. Tickets are capped daily. English tours run twice a day.
Heat
April–May is gentle; by June it's 35°C+. Climb dunes at sunrise or after 5pm.
Onward
Train to Turpan (~10h overnight) or fly to Urumqi (1.5h) and slow-train down to Turpan.
A moment to hold

Top of the Mingsha dune. Light fading. Lake below already in shadow. Just the wind and the soft hum of the sand. Half the desert is silver. The other half is gold.

Chapter SevenVII.

Turpan, the oasis of fire

A depression below sea level, the hottest place in China, ringed by red sandstone mountains. And yet — vines, raisins, sweet melons, two-thousand-year-old underground aqueducts, and a city built entirely of yellow earth.

"In the shade of a vine trellis, with sweet melon and a bowl of cold yoghurt, you understand why every traveller for two thousand years stopped here."
Day 22 · Turpan, −154 m below sea level

Turpan is a small miracle. It sits in the Turfan Depression, the second-lowest point in the world, surrounded by the red sandstone Flaming Mountains — the very ones the Monkey King fought through in Journey to the West. It is the hottest place in China; in summer, 47°C is normal. And yet it has been a thriving oasis for two thousand years, sustained by a network of underground aqueducts called karez — engineered before Christ, dug by hand into the mountainside to bring meltwater from the Tian Shan without losing it to evaporation. Walk through one of the still-functioning karez. Touch the water. It is impossibly cold.

This is also where you cross a cultural threshold. Turpan is Uyghur — the language shifts from Mandarin to Turkic, the food changes from noodles in soup to lamb on flatbread, and the call to prayer suddenly punctuates the afternoon. You are not in inner China anymore. You are in Central Asia, even if the maps still say otherwise.

Why this leg is worth it

Turpan is the moment the trip changes character. After the Buddhist plateau and the Han Chinese desert, suddenly there are minarets, vine arbours, sweet melon, and the gentle Turkic vowels of Uyghur conversation in the bazaar. You can feel Central Asia beginning.

Historical
Jiaohe Ruins

An entire city carved from a single sandstone island between two rivers — no walls were needed, the cliffs were the walls. 2,300 years old, perfectly preserved by the dry air. Walk it at sunrise.

Cultural
Emin Minaret

An 18th-century brick minaret in pale sand colour, decorated with fifteen different geometric patterns of dimensional brickwork. Adjacent mosque still active. Quiet, beautiful, one for the photo album.

Landscape
Grape Valley

Long covered alleys of vines, laneways under green canopies, drying houses with adobe lattice walls where seedless raisins are made. Order the cold yoghurt with raisin honey.

Culinary
Laghman, polo, nan

Hand-pulled noodles topped with stir-fried lamb and peppers (laghman). Saffron-coloured pilaf with mutton, carrots, raisins (polo). Round flatbread baked in clay tonurs (nan). The Uyghur trinity. Eat all three.

Practical · Turpan & Urumqi

Days
2 in Turpan. Optionally 1 in Urumqi (Grand Bazaar, Tianchi Heavenly Lake) — but most travellers skip and go straight to Kashgar.
Heat
April–early June is bearable (25–35°C). Avoid July–August unless you enjoy actual furnaces.
Onward
Sleeper train Turpan → Kashgar overnight (~24h) is a journey in itself, or fly Urumqi → Kashgar (~1.5h, often cheaper than train sleeper).
Note
Xinjiang has more security checkpoints than rest of China. Carry passport always. Stay polite. Move on.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon under a vine trellis in Grape Valley. A bowl of cold yoghurt. The sun behind the Flaming Mountains is the same colour as the rocks. Nothing to do for an hour.

Chapter EightVIII.

Kashgar, the last city of China

The Silk Road's southern hub for fifteen centuries. Adobe alleys, the largest mosque in China, a Sunday animal bazaar where sheep change hands by the thousand, and a day trip up the Karakoram Highway to Karakul Lake.

"Kashgar is the place where the road forks: south through the Karakoram, west over the Pamirs, north toward Russia. Every empire in history has stopped here, and none of them has ever quite owned it."
Day 26 · Kashgar, 1,289 m

Kashgar is the end of the Chinese Silk Road and the beginning of something else. For fifteen hundred years it has been the meeting point of the southern and middle Silk Road branches — the place where a caravan coming from Kashmir met one coming from Samarkand, both restocked, ate, prayed, traded, and continued. It is geographically in China, culturally in Central Asia, and emotionally somewhere of its own.

Walk the Old Town in the early morning, before the bus tours arrive. The adobe houses, the carved wooden doors, the tiny bakery selling samsa hot from a tonur. Pray (or watch) at Id Kah Mosque, the largest in China. On Sunday, take a taxi to the livestock bazaar — it is everything you think a Silk Road bazaar should be: sheep being inspected, donkeys braying, men in doppa caps haggling in Uyghur, the smell of dust and lanolin, fistfuls of yuan changing hands. Old guidebooks were not romanticising; this is real.

Then on a separate day, drive south up the Karakoram Highway. Three and a half hours later you are at Karakul Lake — a high, black, glacier-fed body of water at 3,600 metres, with the 7,546-metre Muztagh Ata reflecting in it. Kyrgyz herders run yurt camps on the shore. Stay one night. Drink salty milk tea. Look at the stars.

Why this leg is worth it

Kashgar is one of perhaps a dozen cities on earth where you can still feel the ancient world in the present tense. The Sunday bazaar alone justifies the detour. And Karakul — well, Karakul is one of those landscapes that does not need a description. You will sit by the water and not say much.

Cultural
Sunday Livestock Bazaar

Weekly since the 10th century. Animals only — sheep, goats, donkeys, the occasional camel. Goes from 7am to early afternoon, peaks 10–11. The whole regional economy compressed into one chaotic field.

Cultural
Old Town & Id Kah

Adobe lanes, carved doors, tonur bakeries. Id Kah Mosque (1442) is the largest in China — visit Friday afternoon to see Kashgar's full Muslim community at prayer.

Landscape
Karakul Lake & Muztagh Ata

3.5h drive south on the Karakoram Highway. A high black lake at 3,600m reflecting a 7,546m peak. Yurt stays on the shore (~€25 pp). The drive itself is half the experience.

Culinary
Lamb everything

Whole-roasted lamb (kawap), lamb laghman, polo, cumin-crusted skewers, samsa pastries. End the meal with sweet apricots, dried mulberries, and pomegranate juice. No fish here. Don't ask for it.

Practical · Kashgar

Days
4–5. Schedule arrival to include a Sunday for the bazaar. Plan one overnight at Karakul.
Stay
Old Town Youth Hostel or a boutique like Kashgar Old City Vintage Hotel. In Karakul, book a yurt through the Kyrgyz family at the lake's edge.
Karakul logistics
Hire a private driver (~€80 round trip) or join a shared minibus. Bring warm clothes; nights below freezing even in May. Border-zone permit is sometimes needed — your hotel handles it.
Checkpoints
Many in Xinjiang. Always carry passport. Be patient and polite. Photographing checkpoints, military, or police is a hard no.
A moment to hold

First light on Karakul. The lake is black glass. Muztagh Ata catches the sun first, turning gold from the summit down. Outside the yurt, a herder is already moving the yaks. You are the only ones awake.

Chapter NineIX.

Across the Irkeshtam Pass

One long day from Kashgar to Osh. A Chinese exit stamp at Ulugqat, a high empty no-man's-land, and then suddenly the Alay Valley opens beneath you and the Pamirs are at your back. Welcome to Central Asia.

"You leave China at a windswept post in the morning and you arrive in Kyrgyzstan by the afternoon — and the world feels suddenly larger and quieter."
Day 32 · Sary-Tash, 3,170 m

The Irkeshtam Pass is the cleanest land border crossing between China and Central Asia, and it is one long, strange, beautiful day. Take a shared taxi from Kashgar's bus station to Ulugqat (Wuqia), about 130 km west — this is where you actually clear Chinese customs, well before the physical border. Have your phone unlocked. Have your bags accessible. Stay polite. The whole thing takes a few hours.

Then through no-man's-land — a windswept stretch where you'll need to find onward transport — and to the Kyrgyz post, where the formalities are blissfully short (Germans get visa-on-arrival, free, 60 days). You emerge into the Alay Valley: a wide, high, green grassland flanked by the Pamirs to the south and the Tian Shan to the north. Stop in Sary-Tash for a night if you can. Have besh barmak (literally "five fingers" — boiled lamb on noodles, eaten by hand) at a homestay. In the morning, the Pamir wall is the entire southern horizon. It is one of the great roadside views in the world.

From Sary-Tash, a shared 4WD descends through the Alay Valley to Osh — Kyrgyzstan's southern capital, three thousand years old, once the great Silk Road waystation between Samarkand and Kashgar. The Jayma Bazaar is still going. Sulaiman-Too — a sacred mountain in the centre of the city — was a place of pilgrimage long before Islam arrived. Climb it at sunset.

Why this leg is worth it

Borders should feel like something. Most don't. This one does. You will remember the Irkeshtam crossing the way some people remember a long flight — the in-between hours, the sense of moving from one civilisation to another, the slow exhalation when the Kyrgyz official hands back your passport with a smile.

Landscape
The Alay Valley

A high green corridor between the Pamirs and the Tian Shan. Wild horses, yurt camps, jagged 7,000 m peaks on the southern wall. Some of the highest pasture land on earth.

Cultural
Osh's Jayma Bazaar

Three kilometres of covered market along the Ak-Buura river. Spices, dried fruits, bread, kumys (fermented mare's milk — try it once), traditional knives. Older than the city around it.

Historical
Sulaiman-Too

The "throne of Solomon" — a 1,100m sacred mountain rising from Osh's centre. UNESCO-listed. Climbed by pilgrims for at least 1,500 years. Sunset from the top is the moment.

Culinary
Plov, manty, lagman

Osh-style plov is the gold standard — saffron rice, lamb, carrots, garlic, raisins, slow-cooked in a kazan. Manty are giant steamed dumplings with lamb and onion. Lagman returns, but with a bolder, tomatoey Kyrgyz hand.

Practical · The Crossing

Border hours
Closed weekends and Chinese/Kyrgyz public holidays. Closed daily 13:00–16:00 Beijing time for lunch. Arrive at Ulugqat before 10am to be safe.
Logistics
Shared taxi Kashgar → Ulugqat ~CNY 35. Taxi or bus through no-man's-land. Onward shared 4WD to Sary-Tash on the Kyrgyz side. Some travellers prefer the once-weekly direct Kashgar → Osh bus.

Epilogue · A Practical Reference

Everything you'll actually need to know.

The romance is in the chapters above. The logistics are below. Permits, money, weather, packing, connectivity — the unglamorous backbone that lets the rest of the trip feel effortless.

Timing

  • Best window: Mid-April to mid-June. Tibet's clearest weather, mild temps in the desert, snow-free Pamir passes.
  • Tibet closes to foreigners late February through March each year. Plan around it.
  • Avoid July–August in Turpan (50°C is real) and during Chinese Golden Week (Oct 1–7) when domestic tourism explodes.
  • Irkeshtam border: closed weekends and holidays year-round, often by snow Nov–April.

Visas & Permits

  • China: Germans get 30 days visa-free in 2026. For a longer stay, a regular L tourist visa is straightforward.
  • Tibet Travel Permit: mandatory regardless of visa-free status. Only obtainable through a licensed agency, ~4–6 weeks lead time.
  • Chinese Group Visa: required if entering Tibet from Kathmandu. Issued in Kathmandu, 4 working days.
  • Kyrgyzstan: Germans visa-free 60 days. No paperwork.

Budget Estimate

  • Tibet tour (8 days): €1,000–1,500 pp
  • Lhasa (4 days): €300–500 for two
  • Sky Train soft sleeper: €100 pp
  • Silk Road China leg (2 weeks): €1,200–1,800 for two (transport, hotels, food)
  • Kashgar + Karakul (5 days): €400–600 for two
  • Border + Osh (3 days): €150–250 for two
  • Total Kathmandu→Osh: roughly €4,000–5,500 for both of you, mid-range.

Money & Payments

  • China: set up Alipay or WeChat Pay before arriving. Foreign cards work for both via "TourPass." Cash and Visa are nearly useless.
  • Tibet & Xinjiang: bring some RMB cash for taxis and small vendors who haven't gone digital.
  • Kyrgyzstan: ATMs in Osh, Bishkek; cash USD often accepted for big-ticket items. Som for everything else.
  • Tip: not customary in any of these countries. Round up at most.

Connectivity

  • VPN: install before arriving in China. NordVPN or Astrill have the most reliable performance. Without one: no Google, no WhatsApp, no Instagram.
  • SIM cards: a Chinese SIM (China Unicom) works across the country. Switch to a Kyrgyz SIM (Beeline or O!) on arrival in Osh.
  • eSIM alternative: Airalo regional eSIMs cover China + Central Asia and just work.
  • On the train: no WiFi on the Sky Train, patchy signal. Bring downloaded books/films.

Health & Altitude

  • Altitude: the slow Kathmandu→Lhasa ascent acclimatises you well. Pack Diamox as backup.
  • Vaccinations: standard travel jabs (Hep A&B, typhoid, tetanus). Rabies if you'll be near animals at Karakul or Sary-Tash.
  • Water: tap water unsafe everywhere on this route. Filter bottle (Grayl, LifeStraw) or boil.
  • Insurance: needs to cover trekking to 5,500m and emergency evac. Most standard policies don't.

Pack Essentials

  • One down jacket each. Tibet, Karakul and Sary-Tash are cold even in May.
  • Layers: thermal base, mid-weight fleece, light shell. Mix-and-match across 0°C to 35°C.
  • Sun: SPF 50, Cat. 4 sunglasses, wide-brim hat. UV at altitude is brutal.
  • Footwear: one pair of broken-in trail shoes does it all. Leave the heavy boots.
  • Tech: Kindle, offline maps (Maps.me + Organic Maps), portable battery, universal adapter.
  • Documents: 6+ passport photos. Photocopies of every page. Backup copies on encrypted drive.

Three things nobody warns you

  • Showers are rare on the road between Tingri and Lhasa. Wet wipes are gold.
  • Photographing military, police, or checkpoints in Xinjiang: don't. Even casual.
  • Tibetan dogs at high-altitude monasteries can be aggressive. Don't pat. Carry a small stick.
