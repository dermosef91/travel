The Route at a Glance

Nine chapters, three countries,
one ribbon of road and rail.

The trip is a single thread following the spine of Asia: down the Himalayas, across the Tibetan plateau, along the Silk Road, and over the Pamirs into Central Asia. Every leg has its own colour, climate, and cuisine.

PrologueI.

Kathmandu, the threshold

Where the plan becomes a journey. Marigold alleys, butter-lamp dusk, one last hot shower before the plateau.

"You sit on a rooftop in Thamel, the sun goes red behind the hills, and you realise — this is where it begins."
Day 0 · Kathmandu, 1,400 m

Kathmandu is the threshold city — warm, noisy, smelling of marigold and cardamom, where every traveller pauses before walking up. Spend three or four days. Drink masala chai on rooftops. Walk the kora around Boudhanath — three times, slowly, with the grandmothers who have done it every evening for forty years.

This is also where the Tibet permit gets stamped and the Chinese group visa is issued. Use the time. Buy a down jacket. Get cash. Eat one last plate of crispy momo.

Why this leg is worth it

It calibrates you. Everything that follows — the plateau's thin air, the desert silences, the rough comfort of an Osh chaikhana — lands harder because you started in the warm chaos where every alley is a shrine.

Cultural
Boudhanath at dusk

Nepal's largest stupa, ringed by Tibetan monasteries. The kora at sunset, butter lamps lighting one by one, is one of the most quietly moving things you'll see all year.

Historical
Patan Durbar Square

Most beautiful of the three royal squares — Newari woodcarving, Krishna temples, and a museum among Asia's finest. Quieter than Kathmandu's Durbar.

Landscape
Nagarkot dawn

An hour east, a ridge showing the entire Himalayan crest from Annapurna to Everest on clear mornings. Stay overnight, alarm at 5:30. Worth losing the sleep.

Practical · Kathmandu

When to be here
Late March or early April — visa processing takes 4 days at the Chinese embassy.
Stay in
Patan or Boudha, not Thamel. Quieter, more atmospheric.
Spend
~€40–70/night guesthouse; €5–8/meal.
A moment to hold

Last evening at Boudhanath. The stupa's eyes are looking north — the same direction you are about to go.

Chapter TwoII.

Across the Himalayas

Eight days on the Friendship Highway. Gyirong border, Tingri's high steppe, Everest's north face, and Yamdrok Lake's impossible turquoise.

"On the third morning the air thins and the road begins climbing in earnest, and you don't speak much, because there is nothing to say."
Day 3 · Tingri, 4,300 m

This is the leg that earns the trip its title. Eight days from Kathmandu, up through the Bhote Koshi gorge to the Gyirong border, then onto the Tibetan plateau. The colours shift: green to brown, brown to ochre, ochre to white. By Tingri you are at 4,300 metres and the stars are brighter than you knew stars could be.

From Tingri, detour to Rongbuk Monastery and the Tibetan side of Everest Base Camp — at 5,200 metres, the highest you'll probably ever stand. Then back onto the Friendship Highway: Shigatse, Tashilhunpo Monastery, Gyantse, and the high pass at Karo La where Yamdrok Lake opens below, scorpion-shaped and the colour of melted glaciers.

Why this leg is worth it

You will never forget the moment Everest comes out of the cloud. The slow ascent at the pace of a four-wheel-drive is a meditation no plane can give you.

Landscape
Everest North Face

From Rongbuk, the north face stands directly before you — a sheer 3,500 m wall. Sunrise is the time. The cold is brutal and worth it.

Landscape
Yamdrok Lake

One of Tibet's three sacred lakes. The first sight from Karo La pass — the colour of a Florentine sky — is the most photographed view in Tibet for good reason.

Cultural
Tashilhunpo Monastery

Seat of the Panchen Lama, founded 1447. Best in the morning when monks debate in the courtyard — clapping, gesturing, sparring with logic.

Practical · The Crossing

Tour cost
~€1,000–1,500 pp for the 8-day overland (private car, guide, hotels, all permits).
Altitude protocol
Slow ascent, lots of water, no alcohol nights 1–3. Diamox in your kit.
What to pack
Down jacket, thermals, sun protection (UV is brutal), lip balm, wool buff. Layers above all.
A moment to hold

First light at Rongbuk, hands inside your sleeves, breath visible, Everest turning gold from the top down. No words.

Chapter ThreeIII.

Lhasa, the holy city

Three days at 3,656 metres. The Potala on its hill, the Jokhang at dawn, the Barkhor in late afternoon, and the way altitude makes every walk a little holy.

"At dawn the pilgrims circle the Jokhang, prayer wheels turning, robes burgundy in the rising sun, and you are entirely surplus to requirements — which is the point."
Day 9 · Lhasa, 3,656 m

Lhasa is the holy city, the destination of every Tibetan pilgrim's life. You arrive after eight days on the road already half-acclimatised — most travellers fly in and spend their first 24 hours dizzy on a hotel bed. You walk straight into the Barkhor, the pilgrim circuit around the Jokhang, and join its slow clockwise current.

Three days is enough. The Potala Palace — thirteen stories of white-and-red mud-brick rising from the Red Hill — costs an entry slot booked the day before. The Jokhang at dawn, full of prostrating pilgrims and the smell of butter lamps, is its own sacrament. In the afternoons, drink sweet milk tea in a Barkhor tea-house.

Why this leg is worth it

Almost no city on earth is still organised around a single religious heart the way Lhasa is. Walking the Barkhor, you are inside something living — not a museum but a practice ongoing for thirteen centuries.

Cultural
The Jokhang at dawn

Tibet's most sacred temple, founded 7th century. Go at 7am — hundreds of pilgrims doing full-body prostrations on the flagstones. Inside, the Jowo Shakyamuni statue from Tang dynasty China.

Cultural
Sera Monastery debates

Every afternoon (except Sundays), monks debate Buddhist philosophy under the willows, with elaborate hand-claps to punctuate arguments. Free, riveting, theatrical.

Historical
The Potala

13 stories, 1,000+ rooms, 200,000 statues. More powerful from outside, at sunset, with butter lamps catching gold on the roof.

Practical · Lhasa

Days needed
3–4. More if you want Namtso Lake (overnight trip over a 6,000 m pass).
Guide rules
Officially required for tourist sites. Free time in Barkhor and tea-houses is yours.
Stay near
Barkhor area — Yak Hotel or House of Shambhala. Walk everywhere.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon on the Barkhor. The sun catches the gold roofs of the Jokhang. Pilgrims, monks, soldiers, tourists, all moving clockwise. You go with them.

Chapter FourIV.

The Sky Train

21 hours on the highest railway in the world. Lhasa to Xining over the Tanggula Pass at 5,072 metres, through Hoh Xil, in oxygen-fed carriages while the plateau slides past.

"You wake at 4am with the carriage gently rocking, look out the window, and there is the full moon over Hoh Xil with nothing else for two hundred kilometres in any direction."
Lhasa → Xining · Train Z8982 · 1,956 km

The Qinghai-Tibet Railway is the highest in the world — built on permafrost across the coldest, emptiest land on the planet. The carriages are pressurised with an oxygen outlet at every berth.

The Z8982 leaves Lhasa at 09:25 and crosses the Tanggula Pass — 5,072 metres, the highest railway point anywhere — in late afternoon light. By dusk you are in Hoh Xil, where Tibetan antelope and wild yak still roam. You sleep through the descent into Qinghai and wake into dry valleys. By 06:25 you are in Xining and on the Silk Road.

Why this leg is worth it

Trains like this don't exist elsewhere. Not just transport — a long, panoramic farewell to the plateau. Take the soft sleeper. Don't sleep until Hoh Xil is dark.

Landscape
Tanggula Pass

5,072 m — the highest railway point on earth. A weather station, prayer flags, and nothing else. The train pauses; passengers crowd the windows.

Landscape
Hoh Xil & Cona Lake

UNESCO-listed wilderness. Watch for Tibetan antelope, wild ass, and the occasional bear. Cona Lake reflects the sky like polished steel.

Practical
Soft sleeper

Four berths per compartment, a door that closes. ~€100 pp. Hard sleeper (six berths, open) is fine but social. Worth the upgrade for 21 hours.

Practical · The Sky Train

The right train
Z8982 dep. 09:25, arr. Xining 06:25. Daytime over Tanggula. Avoid Z8992 (you sleep through the best scenery).
Booking
Through your Tibet agency with the tour. Foreign passports are clunky on 12306.cn.
Cost
Soft sleeper ~€100, hard sleeper ~€65. No Tibet permit needed to leave — only to enter.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon, somewhere past Tanggula. You and Micky pressed against the window. The plateau gold under low light. A herd of wild ass running parallel to the tracks for half a minute, then gone.

Chapter FiveV.

The Hexi Corridor

The Silk Road's chokepoint between two deserts and two mountain ranges. Bullet trains and rainbow mountains.

"This is the road every empire walked. The Han, the Tang, the Mongols, Marco Polo. You're walking it now, and it has not yet finished."
Xining → Jiayuguan · ~1,500 km

The Hexi Corridor is the Silk Road's defining geography — a narrow strip squeezed between the Tibetan Plateau and the Gobi. For two thousand years, every caravan heading west threaded this needle. So do you, but at 250 km/h.

Pause in Xining for Kumbum Monastery and a bowl of hand-pulled beef noodles. Then west to Zhangye for the rainbow Danxia landforms — striped sandstone ridges impossibly painted in late afternoon light. Onward to Jiayuguan: the western terminus of the Ming Great Wall, where the desert begins.

Why this leg is worth it

Geography becomes legible. You can see why these cities, in this line, mattered. The Hexi Corridor turns the Silk Road from a metaphor into the route you're physically following.

Landscape
Zhangye Danxia

Striped sandstone the colours of a sunset. Layers of red, orange, ochre and white folded over each other across whole hillsides. Late afternoon, with the sun low — that's when it sings.

Historical
Jiayuguan Fort

The "First and Greatest Pass under Heaven" — westernmost guard tower of the Ming Wall. Exiles tossed a stone at the wall as they left; if it bounced back, they'd return. Many didn't.

Cultural
Kumbum Monastery (Ta'er Si)

One of Tibetan Buddhism's six great Gelug monasteries, near Xining. Famous for yak-butter sculptures of staggering intricacy, remade yearly and allowed to melt.

Practical · Hexi Corridor

How to move
High-speed rail throughout. Xining → Zhangye 2.5h, Zhangye → Jiayuguan 2h, Jiayuguan → Dunhuang ~4h.
Days
1 Xining, 1 Zhangye, 1 Jiayuguan — a week including travel days.
Booking trains
Trip.com app works for foreigners. Book 5–7 days ahead. Second-class seats are fine.
A moment to hold

Last light on the striped hills outside Zhangye. The colours don't make sense and you stop trying to make them.

Chapter SixVI.

Dunhuang, where the desert meets the Buddha

An oasis in the Gobi. A thousand years of Buddhist art in sandstone cliffs. A crescent lake against the dunes. The most photogenic stop on the route.

"You climb the dune at sunset, the wind rises, and the lake below catches a ribbon of gold. The sand sings under your feet. This is what oasis means."
Day 18 · Dunhuang

Dunhuang is the heart of the Silk Road. For a millennium it was the crossroads where Buddhist monks, Persian traders, and Sogdian merchants converged. They left behind the Mogao Caves: 492 grottoes carved over a thousand years, every surface painted in mineral pigments still impossibly vivid.

Just outside the city, the Singing Sand Mountains surround a crescent freshwater lake that has not dried up in two thousand years. Rent a camel at golden hour. Climb the highest dune. Watch the lake go from blue to gold to violet.

Why this leg is worth it

Mogao alone is worth the trip — to Buddhist art what Sistine is to Renaissance painting. Add the Crescent Lake, the night market, the apricot wine. Dunhuang earns its three days more easily than anywhere else.

Cultural
Mogao Caves

492 grottoes, 45,000 m² of murals, 2,000 sculptures. You'll see 8 caves on a 2-hour guided tour with timed-entry. Book the English tour. The Library Cave's history (sealed 1000 years, opened 1900) is the subplot.

Landscape
Crescent Lake & Mingsha

A spring-fed lake against 100m sand dunes, kept liquid by aquifer for 2,000 years. Climb the dune at sunset; the sand "sings" (a low rumbling hum) when you slide down.

Historical
Yumen Pass & the Jade Gate

An afternoon's drive west — the western customs post of Han dynasty China. Mud-brick remnants on a windswept plain. Where China ended.

Practical · Dunhuang

Days
3 minimum. One for Mogao, one for the dunes, one for Yumen Pass.
Mogao tickets
Book online 2–7 days ahead. Tickets capped daily. English tours twice a day.
Heat
April–May is gentle; June 35°C+. Climb dunes at sunrise or after 5pm.
A moment to hold

Top of the Mingsha dune. Light fading. Lake below already in shadow. Just the wind and the soft hum of the sand. Half the desert is silver. The other half is gold.

Chapter SevenVII.

Turpan, the oasis of fire

Below sea level, the hottest place in China, ringed by red sandstone mountains. And yet — vines, sweet melons, and two-thousand-year-old underground aqueducts.

"In the shade of a vine trellis, with sweet melon and a bowl of cold yoghurt, you understand why every traveller for two thousand years stopped here."
Day 22 · Turpan, −154 m below sea level

Turpan sits in the Turfan Depression, the second-lowest point in the world, surrounded by the red Flaming Mountains from Journey to the West. In summer, 47°C is normal. And yet it has thrived for two thousand years, sustained by karez — underground aqueducts dug by hand to bring Tian Shan meltwater without losing it to evaporation. Walk through one. Touch the water. Impossibly cold.

This is where you cross a cultural threshold. Turpan is Uyghur — the language shifts to Turkic, the food to lamb on flatbread, the call to prayer punctuates the afternoon. You are in Central Asia now, even if the maps say otherwise.

Why this leg is worth it

The moment the trip changes character. After the Buddhist plateau and Han Chinese desert, suddenly: minarets, vine arbours, sweet melon, and the gentle Turkic vowels of the bazaar. Central Asia beginning.

Historical
Jiaohe Ruins

An entire city carved from a single sandstone island between two rivers — no walls were needed, the cliffs were the walls. 2,300 years old, perfectly preserved by the dry air. Walk it at sunrise.

Landscape
Grape Valley

Covered alleys of vines, drying houses with adobe lattice walls where seedless raisins are made. Order the cold yoghurt with raisin honey.

Culinary
Laghman, polo, nan

Hand-pulled noodles with lamb and peppers (laghman). Saffron pilaf with mutton, carrots, raisins (polo). Flatbread from clay tonurs (nan). The Uyghur trinity.

Practical · Turpan & Urumqi

Days
2 in Turpan. Optionally 1 in Urumqi — but most travellers go straight to Kashgar.
Heat
April–early June is bearable (25–35°C). Avoid July–August.
Note
Xinjiang has more checkpoints than rest of China. Carry passport always. Stay polite.
A moment to hold

Late afternoon under a vine trellis in Grape Valley. A bowl of cold yoghurt. The sun behind the Flaming Mountains is the same colour as the rocks. Nothing to do for an hour.

Chapter EightVIII.

Kashgar, the last city of China

The Silk Road's southern hub for fifteen centuries. Adobe alleys, the largest mosque in China, a Sunday animal bazaar, and Karakul Lake up the Karakoram Highway.

"Kashgar is the place where the road forks: south through the Karakoram, west over the Pamirs, north toward Russia. Every empire in history has stopped here, and none of them has ever quite owned it."
Day 26 · Kashgar, 1,289 m

Kashgar is the end of the Chinese Silk Road and the beginning of something else. For fifteen hundred years it has been where the southern and middle Silk Road branches met — where caravans from Kashmir and Samarkand restocked, traded, and continued. Geographically Chinese, culturally Central Asian, emotionally somewhere of its own.

Walk the Old Town in the early morning. The adobe houses, the carved doors, the bakery selling samsa hot from a tonur. On Sunday, the livestock bazaar is everything a Silk Road bazaar should be: sheep being inspected, men in doppa caps haggling, dust and lanolin, fistfuls of yuan changing hands.

On a separate day, drive south up the Karakoram Highway to Karakul Lake — a high, black, glacier-fed body of water at 3,600 m, with 7,546-metre Muztagh Ata reflecting in it. Yurt camp on the shore. Salty milk tea. Stars.

Why this leg is worth it

One of perhaps a dozen cities where you can still feel the ancient world in the present tense. The Sunday bazaar alone justifies the detour. And Karakul doesn't need a description — you'll sit by the water and not say much.

Cultural
Sunday Livestock Bazaar

Weekly since the 10th century. Animals only — sheep, goats, donkeys, the occasional camel. Goes from 7am to early afternoon, peaks 10–11. The whole regional economy compressed into one chaotic field.

Cultural
Old Town & Id Kah

Adobe lanes, carved doors, tonur bakeries. Id Kah Mosque (1442) is the largest in China — visit Friday afternoon to see Kashgar's full Muslim community at prayer.

Landscape
Karakul Lake & Muztagh Ata

3.5h drive south on the Karakoram Highway. A high black lake at 3,600m reflecting a 7,546m peak. Yurt stays on the shore (~€25 pp). The drive itself is half the experience.

Practical · Kashgar

Days
4–5. Arrive to include a Sunday for the bazaar. Plan one overnight at Karakul.
Stay
Old Town boutique hotel. In Karakul, book a yurt through the Kyrgyz family at the lake.
Karakul logistics
Private driver (~€80 round trip) or shared minibus. Warm clothes — below freezing at night even in May.
A moment to hold

First light on Karakul. The lake is black glass. Muztagh Ata catches the sun first, turning gold from the summit down. Outside the yurt, a herder is already moving the yaks. You are the only ones awake.

Chapter NineIX.

Across the Irkeshtam Pass

One long day from Kashgar to Osh. Exit stamp at Ulugqat, no-man's-land, then the Alay Valley opens and the Pamirs are at your back.

"You leave China at a windswept post in the morning and you arrive in Kyrgyzstan by the afternoon — and the world feels suddenly larger and quieter."
Day 32 · Sary-Tash, 3,170 m

The Irkeshtam Pass is the cleanest land border between China and Central Asia. Shared taxi from Kashgar to Ulugqat (130 km west) — this is where you clear Chinese customs, well before the physical border. Phone unlocked, bags accessible, stay polite.

Through no-man's-land to the Kyrgyz post, where formalities are blissfully short (Germans: visa-free, 60 days). You emerge into the Alay Valley: wide, high, green, flanked by Pamirs and Tian Shan. Stop in Sary-Tash for a night. Have besh barmak at a homestay. In the morning, the Pamir wall is the entire southern horizon.

From Sary-Tash, a shared 4WD descends to Osh — Kyrgyzstan's southern capital, three thousand years old. The Jayma Bazaar is still going. Sulaiman-Too, the sacred mountain in the city centre, was pilgrimage long before Islam. Climb it at sunset.

Why this leg is worth it

Borders should feel like something. This one does — the in-between hours, the sense of moving from one civilisation to another, the slow exhalation when the Kyrgyz official hands back your passport with a smile.

Landscape
The Alay Valley

A high green corridor between the Pamirs and the Tian Shan. Wild horses, yurt camps, jagged 7,000 m peaks on the southern wall. Some of the highest pasture land on earth.

Cultural
Osh's Jayma Bazaar

Three kilometres of covered market along the Ak-Buura river. Spices, dried fruits, bread, kumys (fermented mare's milk — try it once), traditional knives. Older than the city around it.

Historical
Sulaiman-Too

The "throne of Solomon" — a 1,100m sacred mountain rising from Osh's centre. UNESCO-listed. Climbed by pilgrims for at least 1,500 years. Sunset from the top is the moment.

Practical · The Crossing

Border hours
Closed weekends and public holidays. Closed 13:00–16:00 Beijing time. Arrive at Ulugqat before 10am.
Logistics
Shared taxi Kashgar → Ulugqat ~CNY 35. Onward shared 4WD to Sary-Tash on the Kyrgyz side.
Visas
Germans: visa-free 60 days for Kyrgyzstan. China side covered by visa-free entry or tourist visa.
A moment to hold

Standing in Sary-Tash at sunrise, coffee in hand, the entire Pamir wall lit up pink in front of you. China is behind you now. The trip is exactly half over. Everything is still possible.

Epilogue · A Practical Reference

Everything you'll actually need to know.

The romance is in the chapters above. The logistics are below. Permits, money, weather, packing, connectivity — the unglamorous backbone that lets the rest of the trip feel effortless.

Timing

  • Best window: Mid-April to mid-June. Tibet's clearest weather, mild temps in the desert, snow-free Pamir passes.
  • Tibet closes to foreigners late February through March each year. Plan around it.
  • Avoid July–August in Turpan (50°C is real) and during Chinese Golden Week (Oct 1–7) when domestic tourism explodes.
  • Irkeshtam border: closed weekends and holidays year-round, often by snow Nov–April.

Visas & Permits

  • China: Germans get 30 days visa-free in 2026. For a longer stay, a regular L tourist visa is straightforward.
  • Tibet Travel Permit: mandatory regardless of visa-free status. Only obtainable through a licensed agency, ~4–6 weeks lead time.
  • Chinese Group Visa: required if entering Tibet from Kathmandu. Issued in Kathmandu, 4 working days.
  • Kyrgyzstan: Germans visa-free 60 days. No paperwork.

Budget Estimate

  • Tibet tour (8 days): €1,000–1,500 pp
  • Lhasa (4 days): €300–500 for two
  • Sky Train soft sleeper: €100 pp
  • Silk Road China leg (2 weeks): €1,200–1,800 for two (transport, hotels, food)
  • Kashgar + Karakul (5 days): €400–600 for two
  • Border + Osh (3 days): €150–250 for two
  • Total Kathmandu→Osh: roughly €4,000–5,500 for both of you, mid-range.

Money & Payments

  • China: set up Alipay or WeChat Pay before arriving. Foreign cards work for both via "TourPass." Cash and Visa are nearly useless.
  • Tibet & Xinjiang: bring some RMB cash for taxis and small vendors who haven't gone digital.
  • Kyrgyzstan: ATMs in Osh, Bishkek; cash USD often accepted for big-ticket items. Som for everything else.
  • Tip: not customary in any of these countries. Round up at most.

Connectivity

  • VPN: install before arriving in China. NordVPN or Astrill have the most reliable performance. Without one: no Google, no WhatsApp, no Instagram.
  • SIM cards: a Chinese SIM (China Unicom) works across the country. Switch to a Kyrgyz SIM (Beeline or O!) on arrival in Osh.
  • eSIM alternative: Airalo regional eSIMs cover China + Central Asia and just work.
  • On the train: no WiFi on the Sky Train, patchy signal. Bring downloaded books/films.

Health & Altitude

  • Altitude: the slow Kathmandu→Lhasa ascent acclimatises you well. Pack Diamox as backup.
  • Vaccinations: standard travel jabs (Hep A&B, typhoid, tetanus). Rabies if you'll be near animals at Karakul or Sary-Tash.
  • Water: tap water unsafe everywhere on this route. Filter bottle (Grayl, LifeStraw) or boil.
  • Insurance: needs to cover trekking to 5,500m and emergency evac. Most standard policies don't.

Pack Essentials

  • One down jacket each. Tibet, Karakul and Sary-Tash are cold even in May.
  • Layers: thermal base, mid-weight fleece, light shell. Mix-and-match across 0°C to 35°C.
  • Sun: SPF 50, Cat. 4 sunglasses, wide-brim hat. UV at altitude is brutal.
  • Footwear: one pair of broken-in trail shoes does it all. Leave the heavy boots.
  • Tech: Kindle, offline maps (Maps.me + Organic Maps), portable battery, universal adapter.
  • Documents: 6+ passport photos. Photocopies of every page. Backup copies on encrypted drive.

Three things nobody warns you

  • Showers are rare on the road between Tingri and Lhasa. Wet wipes are gold.
  • Photographing military, police, or checkpoints in Xinjiang: don't. Even casual.
  • Tibetan dogs at high-altitude monasteries can be aggressive. Don't pat. Carry a small stick.
